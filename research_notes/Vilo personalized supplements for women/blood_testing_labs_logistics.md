## Q1. Which biomarkers change a supplement formula for women, cycle-timing rules, and a core panel plus life-stage add-ons

### Takeaway
Only a few markers reliably change what goes into a supplement: ferritin plus iron studies (include iron or not, and the dose), 25-OH vitamin D (dose), B12 with or without MMA (dose and form), folate (mostly for conception planning), the omega-3 index (EPA/DHA dose), and the CMP/CBC as safety gates. Major 2024-2026 guidance changes the picture: the 2024 Endocrine Society guideline advises *against* routine vitamin D testing in healthy adults. In contrast, the 2026 ASH iron guideline *raised* the ferritin cutoff for iron deficiency to ≤30 ng/mL (≤50 in some high-risk groups), which makes ferritin the single most actionable test for menstruating women. Sex hormones, cortisol, RBC magnesium and zinc are poor drivers of formulation and mostly diagnostic or confounded.

### Cited Findings
**Iron / ferritin (strongest case for testing)**
- The American Society of Hematology (ASH) 2026 iron-deficiency guideline recommends a ferritin cutoff of ≤30 ng/mL to diagnose iron deficiency in adults, including menstruating and pregnant women. It says ≤50 ng/mL may be considered for selected high-risk groups (published/covered Aug 17, 2026). — [US Pharmacist](https://www.uspharmacist.com/article/ash-guidelines-raise-ferritin-thresholds-for-iron-deficiency/)
- ASH advised against the older ≤15 ng/mL cutoff for menstruating individuals and cited a pooled global iron-deficiency prevalence of 27% (79 studies). — [Contemporary OB/GYN, Sept 18, 2026](https://www.contemporaryobgyn.net/view/faq-ash-iron-deficiency-ferritin-thresholds-diagnosis); also covered by [News-Medical, Oct 9, 2026](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261009/New-iron-deficiency-guidelines-raise-key-blood-test-cutoffs.aspx)
- The AGA 2020 guideline uses ferritin <45 ng/mL in patients *with anemia* (85% sensitivity, 92% specificity). The AGA said the right threshold for people without anemia is uncertain. — [AAFP summary of AGA guideline (2021)](https://aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2021/0800/p211.pdf)
- The WHO's <15 µg/L cutoff is based on expert opinion. A 2025 Lancet Global Health study proposed higher, physiologically based ferritin thresholds for women. — [PubMed 40288394](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40288394/)
- In young women, iron absorption starts to rise below a ferritin of about 50 µg/L (2021 EClinicalMedicine). — [PubMed 34401687](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34401687/)
- CDC published an iron-indicators report in its 2026 nutrition report series. — [CDC Iron Indicators PDF (2026)](https://www.cdc.gov/nutrition-report/media/pdfs/2026/06/Iron-Indicators-508.pdf) (contents not retrieved; verify)

**Vitamin D (actionable for dosing, but guideline headwind against testing)**
- The Endocrine Society's 2024 guideline (JCEM, June 2024; replaces the 2011 guideline) suggests *against* routine 25(OH)D testing in healthy adults under 50, adults 50-74, and during pregnancy. Certainty of evidence is low. — [PubMed 38828931](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38828931/); [Endocrine Society guideline page](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/vitamin-d-for-prevention-of-disease); [MDedge "Don't screen vitamin D"](https://mdedge.com/content/dont-screen-vitamin-d-new-endo-society-guideline)
- Its rationale is that no evidence-based 25(OH)D target levels exist and testing has no proven benefit. Testing is still appropriate for specific indications, such as hypocalcemia or malabsorption including gastric bypass and IBD. — [AAFP 2026 summary](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2026/0300/practice-guidelines-vitamin-d-disease-prevention.html)
- The guideline suggests *empiric* supplementation without testing for pregnant adults, people with prediabetes, ages 1-18 and adults ≥75. — [AAFP 2026 summary](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2026/0300/practice-guidelines-vitamin-d-disease-prevention.html)

**Omega-3 index**
- The target omega-3 index is >8%. Dried blood spot (DBS) results correlate well with RBC-membrane measures. — [Australian Institute of Sport](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements/group_b/other/fish-oils/determining-your-omega-3-index)
- The dose needed to reach a desirable index was modeled from OmegaQuant data (Jackson et al., 2019). — [FoodNavigator-USA, 2019](https://www.foodnavigator-usa.com/Article/2019/01/25/How-much-EPA-DHA-omega-3-do-you-need-for-a-desirable-Omega-3-Index-score); [Nutrients 2019 figure](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6521311/figure/nutrients-11-00817-f002)

**Hormones and cycle timing**
- ZRT's dried-blood-spot kit instructions say to collect on **day 19, 20 or 21** for estradiol, progesterone or testosterone in women with regular cycles. Users must also avoid **biotin** and some facial creams for 3 days beforehand. — [ZRT blood spot instructions (2026)](https://www.zrtlab.com/media/3381/blood-inx-42826.pdf); [ZRT blood spot page](https://www.zrtlab.com/sample-types/blood/)

### Inferences
**Per-marker assessment [Background, unverified unless a citation appears above]**

| Marker | Does it reliably change the formula? | Notes / timing |
|---|---|---|
| Ferritin + iron/TIBC/TSAT | **Yes, strongly.** It decides whether to include iron and at what dose. It also protects iron-replete and postmenopausal women from unneeded iron. | Ferritin rises with inflammation, so pair it with hsCRP. TSAT <20% supports deficiency. Fasting morning draw is best for serum iron. Hold iron supplements about 24h before the draw. ASH 2026 cutoff ≤30 ng/mL (cited). |
| CBC | **Partly.** Hemoglobin and MCV separate iron deficiency (low MCV) from B12/folate deficiency (high MCV). Also a safety screen. | Not cycle-dependent. |
| 25-OH vitamin D | **Yes for dose** (e.g., 1,000 vs 2,000-4,000 IU). But the Endocrine Society 2024 guideline is against routine testing (cited). Expect clinician pushback and possible HSA/insurance friction. | Not cycle-dependent. Also offer empiric 1,000-2,000 IU without testing. |
| B12 (+ MMA reflex) | **Yes** for dose and form, especially for vegans and vegetarians, metformin or PPI users, and after bariatric surgery. | Serum B12 is insensitive in the borderline range (~200-400 pg/mL), so reflex to MMA. Biotin and high-dose B12 supplements distort results. |
| Folate (serum vs RBC) | **Mainly for conception planning.** Folic acid 400 µg is recommended for all women who could become pregnant regardless of level, so testing rarely changes the base formula. RBC folate reflects ~3-4 months; serum folate reflects recent intake. | Prefer RBC folate if testing. |
| RBC Mg vs serum Mg | **No.** Serum Mg is <1% of body stores and insensitive. RBC Mg is poorly standardized and lacks validated cutoffs. Diet and symptom data are probably more useful. | Avoid as a formulation driver; at most an optional add-on. |
| Zinc (plasma) | **Weak.** Confounded by inflammation, time of day, fasting state, hemolysis and contamination. | Optional only. |
| Omega-3 index | **Yes** for EPA/DHA dose. DBS is validated and the target is >8% (cited). | Not cycle-dependent. A natural fit for an at-home DBS add-on. |
| Homocysteine | **Moderate.** A functional B-vitamin (B12, folate, B6) marker. Lowering it with B vitamins hasn't been shown to cut CV events. Useful as a secondary check. | Fasting. |
| TSH (reflex FT4), TPO Ab | **Mostly safety and diagnostic.** Positive TPO argues against high-dose iodine and for physician follow-up. Selenium evidence is mixed. FT3 is rarely useful. | **Biotin interference** with immunoassays: stop biotin about 3 days before (ZRT instruction, cited). Critical, because Vilo's own formula may contain biotin. |
| HbA1c, fasting glucose/insulin (HOMA-IR) | **Moderate.** Can guide metabolic add-ons such as inositol or fiber, but drug-like claims are risky. Iron deficiency can falsely raise HbA1c. Insulin assays aren't standardized, so HOMA-IR is noisy. | Fasting for glucose and insulin. |
| Lipids / ApoB | **Moderate.** Guides omega-3 dosing, fiber/psyllium and plant sterols. Mainly a physician-referral and safety trigger. | Fasting optional for standard lipids. |
| hsCRP | **Interpretive.** Needed to read ferritin and zinc. Not a supplement target. | Retest if >10 mg/L (acute illness). |
| CMP | **Safety gate.** Calcium, eGFR and liver enzymes bound vitamin D, magnesium and other doses. | Fasting preferred. |
| Estradiol, progesterone, LH, FSH | **No** for formulation. Values swing through the cycle and are diagnostic (PCOS, ovulation, menopause staging). | FSH/LH/E2 on cycle days 2-5. Progesterone mid-luteal, about 7 days before expected menses ("day 21" in a 28-day cycle; ZRT says days 19-21, cited). FSH is unreliable for diagnosing perimenopause because of variability. |
| AMH | **No.** It measures ovarian reserve, does not predict natural fertility, and has no supplement link. | Any cycle day. Hormonal contraception lowers it. |
| Total/free testosterone, SHBG, DHEA-S | **Only for a PCOS track.** Could support inositol-type add-ons, but the diagnosis belongs to a clinician. | Early follicular, morning. Oral contraceptives raise SHBG. |
| Cortisol | **No.** A single serum value is not interpretable for "adrenal fatigue," which is not a recognized diagnosis. | Diurnal (7-9 am). |
| Prolactin | **No.** Diagnostic only; elevated results need physician follow-up. | Morning, rested. |

**Proposed panels [Analyst recommendation]**
- **Core panel, all users (venous preferred):** CBC; ferritin + iron/TIBC/TSAT; hsCRP; 25(OH)D; B12 with MMA reflex; RBC folate (or serum folate for cost); TSH with reflex FT4; HbA1c; lipid panel; CMP. Add the **omega-3 index** as a separate DBS kit (e.g., OmegaQuant-type lab), because Quest/Labcorp routine panels don't carry it as standard [Background, unverified].
- **Cycling (not trying to conceive):** core plus an optional homocysteine add-on. Capture contraceptive type, cycle length and heavy menstrual bleeding history, because these change how ferritin, SHBG and hormone results read.
- **Trying to conceive:** core plus RBC folate (required), TPO Ab, and optionally day 2-5 FSH/LH/E2 and AMH *for information only*, with physician referral. The formula should follow prenatal norms (folate, iodine, choline) whatever the labs say.
- **Pregnancy/postpartum:** limit Vilo's role. ACOG anemia screening is clinician-led [Background, unverified]. The Endocrine Society is against routine vitamin D testing in pregnancy (cited). Offer a prenatal-safe formula plus a CBC/ferritin recheck postpartum (heavy blood loss), and TSH/TPO for postpartum thyroiditis with physician review.
- **PCOS-suspected track:** total/free testosterone, SHBG, DHEA-S, fasting glucose/insulin, HbA1c, lipids, all early follicular with clinician involvement.
- **Perimenopause (≈40-55):** core plus ApoB. Ferritin matters because heavy and irregular bleeding is common. Don't use FSH/E2 to "diagnose."
- **Postmenopause:** core plus ApoB. Expect low ferritin to be rare. Testing mainly *stops* unneeded iron, and low ferritin here should trigger a GI-bleed workup referral rather than a supplement. B12 matters more with age and PPI/metformin use. Vitamin D/calcium bound by CMP calcium.

## Q2. Collection methods (venous PSC, mobile phlebotomy, capillary/DBS), validated markers and accuracy limits

### Takeaway
A venous draw at a Quest/Labcorp patient service center (PSC), or by mobile phlebotomist, is the only route that reliably supports the whole core panel (iron studies, CBC, CMP, folate, B12/MMA). Capillary devices validate well for HbA1c and lipids, and DBS validates well for the omega-3 index and some hormones. Ferritin and vitamin D on capillary samples are less settled. The biggest 2026 market change: **Function Health bought Getlabs (April 8, 2026)**, so the leading independent mobile-phlebotomy network now belongs to a direct competitor.

### Inferences
- **Recommended collection architecture:** (1) default to a venous PSC draw (Quest or Labcorp via an ordering API) for the core panel; (2) offer paid or subsidized mobile phlebotomy for convenience; (3) use a DBS kit only for the omega-3 index and convenience retests of HbA1c/lipids/vitamin D; (4) don't run ferritin, CBC, iron studies or CMP on DBS.
- **Vendor concentration risk.** With Getlabs owned by Function, Vilo would be sending users to a competitor's network. Alternatives are Junction's at-home phlebotomy network, Quest's in-home collection, Labcorp's partners, or regional mobile phlebotomy firms [Background; vendor names beyond those cited not verified].
- **Capillary-sample problems [Background, unverified]:** hemolysis (raises potassium, LDH, AST), tissue-fluid dilution, small volume limiting reflex testing, and temperature and transit delays.

- Turnaround times were not sourced for any method. [Background, unverified]: PSC routine chemistry typically 1-3 business days; DBS mail-in labs typically 5-10 days including shipping.

## Q3. Lab ordering infrastructure (Junction, Health Gorilla, Rupa/Fullscript), pricing, and wholesale vs consumer prices

### Takeaway
Junction is the most turnkey "physician order + Quest/Labcorp/BioReference requisition + at-home phlebotomy + results API" option for a consumer app. Lab testing sits in its custom-priced Enterprise tier, and no per-order rates are published. Health Gorilla is a FHIR-based ordering and results network that generally needs Vilo's own clinicians and lab accounts. Rupa Health's ordering portal has **closed** and moved into Fullscript, which is a practitioner storefront rather than an app API. Consumer competitors charge about $199-$499 a year for 100+ biomarkers, which implies a wholesale lab cost well under ~$150 per draw at scale.

### Inferences
- **Build path:** Junction Enterprise (or a similar aggregator) gives the fastest route to physician-ordered Quest/Labcorp/BioReference requisitions, PSC booking, at-home phlebotomy, and webhooked results. Health Gorilla fits if Vilo contracts its own clinician network (e.g., Wheel), holds its own lab accounts, and wants FHIR-native data. Fullscript isn't designed as an embedded consumer-app API.
- **Implied economics:** Function charges ~$365/year for two draws of 100+ markers, including phlebotomy, clinician review and the app. Its per-draw lab cost at Quest is therefore plausibly in the tens of dollars to low hundreds. Vilo's ~10-12-test core panel should sit at the low end once negotiated. Treat this as an inference, not a quote.
- **[Background, unverified]** A useful wholesale benchmark is the CMS Clinical Laboratory Fee Schedule (CLFS). Many core tests (CBC 85025, CMP 80053, lipid 80061, HbA1c 83036, ferritin 82728, TSH 84443) reimburse in roughly the $7-$25 range each; 25(OH)D (82306) is roughly $30-$40. ## Q4. Physician oversight: who orders, telehealth/physician-network partners, costs, result review, critical values, CPOM

### Takeaway
In most states and for most labs, a licensed clinician must order the tests. Consumer platforms (Function, Superpower, Junction) use an affiliated or partner physician network that issues the order and reviews results. Partner networks (Wheel, OpenLoop, Beluga, SteadyMD) don't publish pricing. The only 2026 benchmarks found are an OpenLoop proposal at **$9,000 implementation + $1,500/month** and competitor-advertised rates of **~$25 per completed consult**.

### Cited Findings
- Wheel lists no per-consult fee; enterprise deals are quoted individually. — [Tessic Health: Wheel alternatives](https://tessichealth.com/compare/wheel-alternatives)
- One reviewed OpenLoop proposal quoted $9,000 implementation and $1,500/month (as of Sept 4, 2026). In that proposal OpenLoop kept 50-59% of maintenance payments through its own merchant account. — [Cuvo: OpenLoop review](https://cuvo.co/compare/cuvo-vs-openloop); [Cuvo: OpenLoop alternatives](https://cuvo.co/blog/openloop-alternatives) (competitor-authored; treat with caution)
- Beluga publishes its structure (flat monthly access fee, per-visit fees, pass-through pharmacy) but not the numbers. SteadyMD quotes per engagement. — [Cuvo: Beluga alternatives](https://cuvo.co/blog/beluga-health-alternatives); [Tessic: OpenLoop alternatives](https://tessichealth.com/compare/openloop-alternatives)
- Cuvo and Tessic both advertise $25 per completed consult with no revenue share. These are vendors' own prices, not market rates. — [Cuvo provider networks](https://cuvo.co/blog/best-telehealth-provider-networks); [Tessic best platforms](https://tessichealth.com/compare/best-hormone-trt-platforms)
- Health Gorilla + Wheel partnership gives Wheel clinicians diagnostic ordering. — [Health Gorilla](https://www.healthgorilla.com/blog/health-gorilla-partners-with-wheel-to-extend-diagnostic-ordering-through-wheels-virtual-care-ecosystem)
- Function relies on standing orders from its own clinician network. A Medical News Today-branded piece claims NY/NJ/RI require a physician licensed in that state to order labs (secondary, unverified; the domain is medicalnewstoday.us, not the main MNT site). — [medicalnewstoday.us](https://medicalnewstoday.us/?p=4287)
- Patients can get completed test reports directly from CLIA labs under the 2014 CMS-2319-F rule (CLIA/HIPAA). It preempts contrary state laws except those granting more access. — [ACP summary of CMS-2319-F](https://www.acponline.org/practice-career/regulatory-resources/regulatory-compliance/summary-of-clia-programs-and-hipaa-privacy-rule-patients-access-to-test-reports-cms-2319-f-final)

### Inferences
- **Operating model [Analyst recommendation]:** contract a physician network licensed in all 50 states that (a) issues orders under an asynchronous protocol based on intake, (b) reviews every result, (c) handles critical-value callbacks and abnormal-result outreach, and (d) signs off on any lab-driven formula change that crosses clinical thresholds (e.g., iron doses for ferritin <30, vitamin D >2,000 IU). Budget per-order review in the tens of dollars (benchmark $25/consult) plus platform fees.
- **Critical values [Background, unverified]:** CLIA (42 CFR 493.1291) requires the lab to notify the ordering clinician promptly of critical or "panic" values. That clinician is responsible for patient follow-up, so the network contract must spell out 24/7 coverage and an escalation SLA. Examples relevant to Vilo: hemoglobin <7 g/dL, calcium or potassium extremes, glucose >400 mg/dL, very high TSH.
- **Corporate practice of medicine [Background, unverified]:** states such as CA, TX, NY and NJ bar lay corporations from employing physicians or controlling clinical judgment. The usual structure is a management services organization (MSO) with a physician-owned professional corporation, or a white-label network (Wheel/OpenLoop/Beluga/SteadyMD) whose own PCs hold the patient relationship. Vilo shouldn't let formulation algorithms override the ordering physician's judgment on abnormal results.

### Gaps
- No primary price lists from Wheel, OpenLoop, Beluga or SteadyMD. Benchmarks come from competitor-authored comparison pages.
- No primary legal source was reviewed on CPOM or critical-value duties. Attorney review is needed.

## Q5. State restrictions on direct-to-consumer lab testing, CLIA, and whether the app needs lab licensure

### Takeaway
Physician-ordered testing through a telehealth network works in nearly every state. The real friction is in **NY, NJ and RI**: Quest and Labcorp won't fill direct-access or reseller orders there, so platforms route to **BioReference** or exclude those states. **MD and MA** (Labcorp) and **AZ** (Quest, per resellers) also show restrictions. Function and Superpower now serve NY/NJ (Superpower at a higher $399 price). RI remains excluded by every source. The FDA's LDT rule is dead (vacated March 31, 2025; formally removed Sept 19, 2025), so CLIA plus state lab licensure governs the labs, not the app.

### Inferences
- **Launch footprint [Analyst recommendation]:** launch with physician-ordered testing in all states where Quest/Labcorp accept network-physician orders. Route NY/NJ through BioReference (via Junction or a direct account) and exclude RI at launch, matching Superpower and Function. Budget higher costs for NY/NJ (Superpower charges +$50 there).
- **App licensure [Background, unverified]:** an app that only facilitates physician-ordered tests performed by CLIA-certified labs doesn't need its own CLIA certificate. CLIA attaches to the entity performing testing. However: (a) **New York** requires every lab testing NY residents' specimens to hold a NY State Clinical Laboratory Evaluation Program (CLEP) permit, which matters when picking DBS/omega-3 labs; (b) some states (e.g., CA, NV, LA, WA) license phlebotomists, and a few license collection stations, which matters if Vilo runs its own draws; (c) Maryland historically required out-of-state labs serving MD residents to hold an MD permit. Verify each with counsel.
- FDA LDT vacatur means DBS/capillary tests from CLIA labs (e.g., BetterWay's modified-FDA-cleared LDTs, ZRT DBS assays) face no FDA premarket hurdle. Validation quality therefore depends on each lab's CLIA/CAP accreditation, so Vilo should demand method-comparison data.

## Q6. Data flow: vendor APIs, HL7/FHIR/LOINC, PDF upload with OCR/LLM parsing, and patient-portal import

### Takeaway
Results arrive most cleanly through the ordering vendor's API (Junction webhooks, Health Gorilla FHIR DiagnosticReport/Observation), with LOINC-coded values and PDFs. For outside labs, there are two routes: patient-mediated FHIR pulls (Apple Health Records via HealthKit, or aggregators such as 1upHealth and Flexpa), and user-uploaded PDFs parsed by LLMs. Vendor benchmarks put LLM lab-report parsing at roughly 84-94% field-level accuracy. That is too low to dose iron or vitamin D without a human-in-the-loop confirmation step.

### Cited Findings
- Health Gorilla describes its lab network API as FHIR-based (orders and results). — [Health Gorilla Lab Network](https://www.healthgorilla.com/home/products/lab-network)
- Apple Health Records requires providers to expose FHIR R4 US Core (v3.1.1) or Argonaut DSTU2 endpoints with SMART on FHIR OAuth. Required resources include DiagnosticReport and Observation (labs and vital-signs categories). Health Records integrations run through EHR vendors serving the US, Canada or UK. — [Apple: technical requirements](https://support.apple.com/guide/healthregister/apd12d144779/web); [Apple: vendor application](https://support.apple.com/guide/healthregister/apd52ad9841c/web); [Apple Health Records overview](https://apple.com/healthcare/health-records/)
- 1upHealth keeps an approved list of consumer apps (including **Flexpa**) for its CMS Patient Access API endpoints. Apps must prove they can consume R4 FHIR and attest to privacy/security. Access is read-only, per member, and consent-based. — [1upHealth third-party apps](https://docs.1up.health/help-center/Content/en-US/get-started/cms-patient-access/third-party-apps.html)
- **LLM/OCR parsing:** Eka Care (vendor) reports lab-report parsing scores of 0.942 for its own model vs 0.836 for GPT-4o and 0.879 for Claude 3.5 Sonnet on PDFs and phone photos. It says it maps 1,400+ investigations to LOINC (self-reported). — [Eka Care developer docs](https://developer.eka.care/eka-medai/technologies/lab-report). Reducto extracts verbatim test names, values, units and reference ranges and does **not** assign LOINC codes, leaving that to a downstream layer. — [Reducto](https://llms.reducto.ai/clinical-lab-report-extraction)
- Analogous peer-reviewed work on pathology reports (not labs) reports near-human structured extraction, e.g., GPT-4o ~96% vs a human annotator ~95% on breast histopathology fields (attribution among these two links uncertain). — [PubMed 40608403](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40608403/); [PubMed 41286063](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41286063/)

### Inferences
- **Recommended pipeline [Analyst recommendation]:**
  1. Native orders: ingest vendor webhook results as JSON/FHIR Observations keyed by LOINC, store the original PDF, and record the performing lab, CLIA number, reference range and units.
  2. Outside results: offer Apple Health Records/HealthKit clinical-record import on iOS and a FHIR aggregator (1upHealth, Flexpa, or Health Gorilla's patient-access/TEFCA services) for portals.
  3. PDF upload: LLM extraction, then unit normalization and plausibility checks, then a mandatory user confirmation screen, then clinician review before any value drives a dose change.
- **Parsing risks [Background, unverified]:** unit confusion (vitamin D ng/mL vs nmol/L, a factor of ~2.5; B12 pg/mL vs pmol/L), lab-specific reference ranges, mis-dated results, and stale results older than 6-12 months. Liability rises if a parsed value auto-changes iron or vitamin D doses without confirmation.
- [Background, unverified] Quest/Labcorp deliver results to ordering systems mainly as HL7 v2 ORU messages, which aggregators translate to JSON/FHIR. HealthKit exposes FHIR lab records the user downloaded via `HKClinicalRecord` (iOS), with user permission.

### Gaps
- Particle Health's consumer-access offering and Flexpa's current lab-result coverage were not confirmed. Payer-sourced claims data often lacks lab values, which would be a limit for Flexpa. Unverified.
- No peer-reviewed accuracy study of LLM parsing of consumer lab PDFs with LOINC mapping was found.
- Junction's results payload format (LOINC fields, PDF) is unconfirmed because the docs couldn't be fetched.

## Q7. Retest cadence per marker

### Takeaway
Retest at about 3 months for most supplement-responsive markers (ferritin, vitamin D, B12, HbA1c), and at about 4 months for the omega-3 index. That fits a quarterly retest bundled with subscription formula updates, with a full panel once a year.

### Cited Findings
- Omega-3 index: intake must be consistent for at least about 4 months to reach a new steady state (Jackson et al. 2019, OmegaQuant data). One worksite study retested at 3 months. — [FoodNavigator-USA](https://www.foodnavigator-usa.com/Article/2019/01/25/How-much-EPA-DHA-omega-3-do-you-need-for-a-desirable-Omega-3-Index-score); [Nutrients 2023 table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10343224/table/nutrients-15-02821-t001)

### Inferences
- **Suggested cadence [Background, unverified, needs citation]:**
  - **Ferritin/iron:** 8-12 weeks after starting or changing iron, then every 6-12 months once replete; sooner with heavy menstrual bleeding.
  - **25(OH)D:** about 12 weeks after a dose change (steady state about 3 months), then yearly (end of winter is most informative).
  - **B12 ± MMA:** 8-12 weeks after starting.
  - **RBC folate:** about 3-4 months (RBC lifespan about 120 days).
  - **Omega-3 index:** 4 months (cited).
  - **HbA1c:** 3 months.
  - **Lipids/ApoB:** 6-12 weeks after a change, then yearly.
  - **Homocysteine:** 2-3 months.
  - **TSH:** 6-8 weeks after a thyroid-relevant change, otherwise yearly.
  - **CBC/CMP:** yearly, or with ferritin/iron rechecks.
- **Product implication:** a "baseline + 3-month follow-up + annual" cadence, with a cheaper targeted retest (ferritin + vitamin D + omega-3 index) at month 3-4, fits supplement response times. Competitors' twice-yearly full draws (Function, Superpower) are a reference point.

### Gaps
- No primary sources were retrieved for ferritin, vitamin D, B12 or folate retest intervals (NIH ODS, ARUP and Mayo couldn't be accessed).

## Q8. Cost and conversion: share of users who complete a draw, and how competitors subsidize or bundle testing

### Takeaway
No published consumer-health figure was found for order-to-draw completion. Adjacent data suggest 60-75% kit return with good reminders, and kit non-return (not failed collection) is the main point of loss. Competitors bundle testing into $199-$499 annual memberships with two draws, use at-home phlebotomy for convenience (Function bought Getlabs), and monetize through supplement and Rx upsells.

### Cited Findings

- Getlabs lets partners subsidize or pass through the convenience fee. — [getlabs.com/api](https://getlabs.com/api)
- Bundled pricing: Function about $365/year with two draws; InsideTracker $489 first year then $149/year plus $340 per test; Superpower $199→$349 (Sept 2026, two draws), $399 in NY/NJ (see Q3 sources). — [Empirical Health](https://www.empirical.health/compare/function-health-vs-superpower-vs-insidetracker/); [Superpower FAQ](https://superpower.com/faqs)

### Inferences
- Plan for roughly 50-75% of users who opt into testing to actually complete a draw. Expect the low end for self-scheduled PSC visits without reminders, and higher with booked at-home phlebotomy or prepaid kits plus reminder sequences. This is an inference from adjacent data; validate in a pilot.
- **Subsidy design:** fold the baseline test cost into a 3-6-month supplement commitment (e.g., "test included with a 3-month plan") rather than selling testing standalone. Make testing optional, with a questionnaire-only formula path, because the Endocrine Society discourages routine vitamin D testing and many users won't draw.

### Gaps
- No disclosed draw-completion rates from Function, Superpower, InsideTracker, Everlywell or similar (none found; no S-1 data).
- Payback and CAC data for test-included bundles weren't found.

## Q9. HSA/FSA eligibility of lab tests

### Takeaway
Lab fees that are "part of medical care" are generally HSA/FSA-eligible under IRS rules. Labcorp OnDemand, Everlywell and InsideTracker market many wellness tests as eligible, but eligibility of purely wellness-motivated panels depends on the plan administrator and documentation. A physician order and itemized receipt strengthen the case. Supplements themselves generally aren't eligible without a practitioner's recommendation for a diagnosed condition.

### Cited Findings
- Guides cite IRS Publication 502 treating laboratory fees that are part of medical care as qualified expenses. Routine blood panels and hormone tests are "usually" payable with HSA/FSA funds. — [Mito Health HSA/FSA guide](https://mitohealth.com/guide/hsa-fsa-eligible-health-tests); [Doctronic](https://www.doctronic.ai/blog/hsa-fsa-lab-tests/)
- Labcorp OnDemand says many tests are HSA/FSA-eligible, including wellness, nutrition and hormone tests. — [Labcorp OnDemand HSA/FSA](https://www.ondemand.labcorp.com/hsa-fsa); [Labcorp OnDemand blog](https://www.ondemand.labcorp.com/blog/how-fsa-and-hsa-funds-can-help-you-stay-healthy); Everlywell similarly. — [Everlywell](https://www.everlywell.com/blog/news-and-info/using-fsa-hsa-for-everlywell-tests/); InsideTracker. — [InsideTracker HSA guide](https://www.insidetracker.com/a/articles/a-guide-to-understanding-and-using-your-hsa-hra-or-fsa)
- HealthEquity says Function lab testing "may qualify" depending on IRS rules and plan terms; the member must confirm. — [HealthEquity](https://healthequity.com/library/lab-testing-function-health-insights)
- A narrower professional reading (NANP) says lab testing must involve a licensed medical professional to be reimbursable. — [NANP](https://nanp.org/?p=34820)
- The IRS keeps an FAQ on medical expenses for nutrition, wellness and general health. — [IRS FAQ](https://www.irs.gov/es/individuals/frequently-asked-questions-about-medical-expenses-related-to-nutrition-wellness-and-general-health)

### Inferences
- [Background, unverified, check against the IRS FAQ] The IRS treats dietary supplements as medical expenses only when a medical practitioner recommends them to treat a specific diagnosed condition. So Vilo's monthly supplement generally isn't HSA-eligible, but the lab test component can be. Selling testing as a separately itemized, physician-ordered line item, perhaps through an HSA/FSA checkout provider (e.g., Flex/Truemed-style letters of medical necessity), would let users pay with pre-tax funds.

### Gaps
- Current Publication 502 text wasn't read directly. No 2025-2026 IRS guidance specific to wellness lab panels was found.
